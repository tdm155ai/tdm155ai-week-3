## 0. The argument of the day (ten theses)

1. **The camera is old; recording is new.** The camera as an optical instrument (dark chamber + aperture/lens + image plane) runs from antiquity through c. 1550 (first lenses) to now. Chemical capture inside it lasts c. 1826–2000s; the bare digital sensor as the only thing at the image plane, ~25 years. For most of the camera's life the thing at the image plane was a screen and the thing recording was a hand.
2. **The original camera was a two-stage system.** Stage one: lens projects an image onto a screen (paper, wall, ground glass). Stage two: a separate agent outside the optical path — a draughtsman — makes the durable record. Film and sensors *collapsed* the two stages by placing the recording medium at the image plane. That collapse is the historical anomaly.
3. **Our activity restores the two-stage system.** Lens → frosted acrylic → phone camera focused on the acrylic. The phone stands where the draughtsman stood. This is a structural claim, not a metaphor.
4. **After tracing came painting; after capture comes processing.** The 17th-century artist traced, then painted (color, tone, invention). We capture, then invert, apply curves, composite. Same architecture, different idiom. The record of the projection is raw material, not the finished work.
5. **The stage-two agent is never neutral.** The tracer chose line weight and omissions; the phone exposes for the hot spot, white-balances the acrylic, records frosting texture and tilt. The record is of the *projection*, with its material signature, not of the world.
6. **Anti-hylomorphism (Ingold).** Form does not pre-exist in the artist's head and get stamped onto passive matter. Form emerges in the friction between maker, tool, and material. The camera does not "capture the photographer's vision"; the photographer, camera, light, and surface *correspond*, and something arrives that none of them fully intended.
7. **Experimentation is the method, not a warm-up.** You find out what the system does by pushing it: sliding the screen, tilting it, half-covering the lens, shooting the acrylic from an angle. Knowledge is itinerant (Ingold), not applied.
8. **Noise is a resource.** Vignetting, blur, hot spots, frosting grain, light leaks, tape hiss, feedback — the system's resistance — becomes the aesthetic material. Eno's studio as instrument, Hendrix's feedback, the Beatles' backwards tape, glitch art. Failure modes are where a medium reveals itself.
9. **Recursion makes the apparatus visible.** Camera pointed at its own monitor (mise-en-abyme, video feedback, Lucier's room) shows the system's transfer function — what it adds on each pass — as image. Every stage of copying is a stage of transformation.
10. **This is the frame for the AI-era course.** The one-stage "camera as truth-recorder" produced an ideology (indexicality, photo-as-evidence) that generative imaging dissolves. Diffusion models literally begin from noise and denoise toward an image. Every AI pipeline is stage two without stage one's optics. Students should feel the pre-indexical camera in their bodies before they meet the post-indexical one.

**One-line version for students:** *For 250 years a camera showed you a picture and someone else had to make the copy. Film briefly let the machine make its own copy. Today we go back to the older arrangement — and pay attention to what the copyist adds.*

## 1. Theoretical spine — Concepts tab

### Hylomorphism and its critics
- **Hylomorphism** — Aristotle: *hyle* (matter) + *morphe* (form). Things are matter given form. Translated into a theory of making: the artisan imposes a preconceived design on inert material. The default folk theory of art and of photography ("capturing a vision").
- **Tim Ingold, *Making* (2013)** — core text. Making as a process of *growth*, following the materials; "form-finding" over "form-giving." Chapters on the hand, the axe, the kite, drawing. Also **"The Textility of Making"** (*Cambridge Journal of Economics*, 2010) — the essay version; **"Materials against Materiality"** (2007); ***Correspondences*** (2020) — making as correspondence with materials rather than interaction with objects.
- **Ingold's key terms** — *itineration* (wayfaring, following) vs. *iteration* (executing a plan); *anticipatory foresight* (the maker sees ahead but does not predetermine); *correspondence*; *the textility of making* (weaving as the model, not architecture); *materials vs. materiality*; *flows and transformations*.
- **Gilbert Simondon** — *Du mode d'existence des objets techniques* (1958); *L'individu et sa genèse physico-biologique* (1964). The brick-and-mould example: hylomorphism hides the labour and the clay's own tendencies inside the mould. *Individuation*, *technicity*, *concretization*. Ingold's main source.
- **Deleuze & Guattari, *A Thousand Plateaus* (1980)** — "Treatise on Nomadology": matter-flow, the artisan as one who *follows* the material (the woodworker following the grain), machinic phylum, "singularities" in matter.
- **Andrew Pickering, *The Mangle of Practice* (1995)** — scientific practice as a dance of resistance and accommodation between human and material agency. Directly transferable to the studio.
- **David Pye, *The Nature and Art of Workmanship* (1968)** — *workmanship of risk* (the outcome is continually at stake during making, e.g., freehand carving) vs. *workmanship of certainty* (the outcome is predetermined, e.g., printing). Perfect vocabulary for the day: the acrylic camera is workmanship of risk; a phone snapshot is workmanship of certainty.
- **Richard Sennett, *The Craftsman* (2008)** — material consciousness, the value of resistance and ambiguity, "the intimate connection between hand and head."
- **Lambros Malafouris, *How Things Shape the Mind* (2013)** — Material Engagement Theory; the potter's wheel example.
- **Karen Barad** — *agential realism*, *intra-action* (*Meeting the Universe Halfway*, 2007). Optional; heavier.

### Photography theory that fits the frame
- **Vilém Flusser, *Towards a Philosophy of Photography* (1983)** — the *apparatus* and its *program*; the photographer as *functionary* who nonetheless "plays against the camera" to produce images not in the program. Almost a manifesto for the day. Also his notion of the *technical image*.
- **Jonathan Crary, *Techniques of the Observer* (1990)** — the camera obscura as the dominant model of vision and subjectivity in the 17th–18th c., displaced in the 19th c. by physiological optics (afterimages, stereoscopes). Key for why the camera obscura mattered *philosophically* (Locke, Descartes, Newton all use it).
- **Walter Benjamin** — "Little History of Photography" (1931): the *optical unconscious*; "The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction" (1935/36): aura, reproducibility.
- **André Bazin, "The Ontology of the Photographic Image" (1945)** — the indexical, "automatic" image; the position the day complicates.
- **Roland Barthes, *Camera Lucida* (1980)** — *that-has-been*, *punctum/studium*; note the title borrows the drawing device.
- **Susan Sontag, *On Photography* (1977)** — "to photograph is to appropriate."
- **Hito Steyerl, "In Defense of the Poor Image" (e-flux, 2009)** — the degraded, copied, compressed image as a class of its own. Directly relevant to rephotographing a screen.
- **Friedrich Kittler, *Gramophone, Film, Typewriter* (1986)** — media determine our situation; noise as the real that media record whether we like it or not.
- **Michel Serres, *The Parasite* (1980)** — noise (*parasite* in French = static) as the third that makes communication possible. **Jacques Attali, *Noise* (1977)** — noise as prophecy of new social orders.
- **Claude Shannon, "A Mathematical Theory of Communication" (1948)** — signal, channel, noise, redundancy. The technical origin of "noise" as a concept.
- **Norbert Wiener, *Cybernetics* (1948)**; **Gregory Bateson, *Steps to an Ecology of Mind* (1972)** — feedback as a general principle. Ties the recursion activity to the noise theme.
- **Marshall McLuhan** — "the medium is the message"; the content of any medium is another medium (a phone photo of a projection is a picture of a picture).

### Working concepts to define as pages
- Two-stage vs. one-stage capture
- Indexicality / index (Peirce) and the "indexical era" of photography (c. 1826–2000s)
- Image plane as the site of contest between recording and interpreting

- Affordance (Gibson) — what a material offers a maker
- Creative misuse / détournement of tools
- Emergence; generative systems (Eno's sense)

## 2. History of the camera obscura — People and Devices tabs

### Antiquity and the medieval world
- **Mozi** (c. 470–391 BCE) — earliest known description of the pinhole image; "collecting place" / "locked treasure room."
- **Aristotle**, *Problemata* — crescent images of a partial eclipse through leaves and wicker.
- **Anthemius of Tralles** (6th c.) — used a camera-obscura-like setup in optics experiments *(verify details)*.
- **Ibn al-Haytham (Alhazen)**, *Kitāb al-Manāẕir* / *Book of Optics* (c. 1011–21) — first rigorous experimental account; multiple lamps through a small hole; establishes that light travels in straight lines and that the image is inverted. Foundational.
- **Shen Kuo**, *Dream Pool Essays* (1088) — describes the inverted image and compares it to an oar's fulcrum.
- **Roger Bacon** (13th c.), **John Pecham**, **Witelo** — Latin transmission of Alhazen; eclipse observation.
- **Levi ben Gershon** (1342) — pinhole for astronomical measurement.

### Renaissance: the lens arrives, drawing begins
- **Leon Battista Alberti** (1435, *De pictura*) — the *velo* (veil/grid) for perspective drawing; also reports a "demonstration" box that may have been a camera obscura or peepshow.
- **Leonardo da Vinci** (c. 1490, Codex Atlanticus; later Manuscript D) — clear descriptions of the camera obscura and its analogy to the eye.
- **Albrecht Dürer** (1525, *Underweysung der Messung*) — the perspective-drawing woodcuts (the "window" and the "sighting device"). Drawing machines before the camera obscura became one.
- **Gemma Frisius** (1545, *De Radio Astronomica et Geometrica*) — first published illustration of a camera obscura: a room used to observe the eclipse of 24 January 1544. Pinhole, no lens.
- **Gerolamo Cardano** (1550, *De subtilitate*) — first published suggestion of a biconvex lens in the aperture.
- **Daniele Barbaro** (1568, *La pratica della perspettiva*) — lens *plus* a stop (diaphragm) to sharpen the image; explicitly recommends it for drawing. The birth of the aperture.
- **Friedrich Risner** (1572, published 1606) — proposes a portable drawing hut with lenses in each wall projecting onto a paper cube in the center, carried like a litter. Ancestor of the curtained box.
- **Giovanni Battista della Porta** (*Magia Naturalis*, 1558; expanded 1589 with a lens) — popularizes the device for drawing and as spectacle; often (wrongly) credited as inventor.
- **Johannes Kepler** (1604, *Ad Vitellionem Paralipomena*) — coins the term *camera obscura*; explains the retinal image; in 1620 used a portable tent camera obscura for landscape drawing (reported by Sir Henry Wotton). Astronomer as draughtsman.

### Baroque: the inhabitable camera and the reflex box
- **Athanasius Kircher**, *Ars Magna Lucis et Umbrae* (1646) — engraving of a room-sized portable cube with lenses in each wall and an inner translucent layer for the artist to draw on, entered by a trapdoor. **The single best image for the day.** Also magic lantern material.
- **Johann Zahn**, *Oculus Artificialis Teledioptricus* (1685) — the most prolific illustrator of the camera obscura; designs a portable reflex box (45° mirror onto a horizontal ground glass) — an SLR with no film, 150 years early. Coins *teledioptrica*. Public Domain Review has the plates.
- **Robert Hooke** — portable "cone" camera for drawing (c. 1694; published posthumously 1726).
- **Robert Boyle**, **Christiaan Huygens**, **Isaac Newton** — box cameras and lens experiments; Newton's *Opticks* prism work uses a darkened room.

### 18th century: the painter's tool
- **Canaletto** (1697–1768) — Museo Correr, Venice, holds a camera obscura attributed to him; his *scaraboti* (sketches) show camera-like perspective.
- **Bernardo Bellotto** — nephew; Dresden and Warsaw views.
- **Sir Joshua Reynolds** — his folding book-shaped camera obscura is in the Science Museum, London.
- **Johannes Vermeer** (1632–75) — the great debate: **Philip Steadman, *Vermeer's Camera* (2001)**; **David Hockney, *Secret Knowledge* (2001)** and the **Hockney–Falco thesis**; **Tim Jenison, *Tim's Vermeer* (2013)** — Jenison builds a mirror-comparator device and repaints *The Music Lesson*. Essential Vermeer website has the full survey.
- **Claude glass** — small tinted convex mirror used by picturesque tourists to compose landscapes; a "pre-processing" filter.
- **Camera lucida** (William Hyde Wollaston, 1806) — prism device that superimposes the scene on the drawing surface; not a dark chamber; Barthes borrows the name.
- **Physionotrace** (Gilles-Louis Chrétien, 1786) — mechanical profile-tracing device; industrialized portraiture before photography.
- **Magic lantern** — the camera obscura reversed (projection); Kircher, Huygens (c. 1659), Zahn; the ancestor of the projector in the recursion activity.

### 19th century: the screen replaced by a light-sensitive surface
- **Thomas Wedgwood & Humphry Davy** (1802) — images on silver-nitrate paper that could not be fixed.
- **Nicéphore Niépce** (1826/27) — *View from the Window at Le Gras*, bitumen on pewter, ~8-hour exposure; earliest surviving camera photograph.
- **Louis Daguerre** (1839) — daguerreotype; the legend of the broken mercury thermometer revealing latent images (probably apocryphal, but a serendipity story).
- **William Henry Fox Talbot** — "photogenic drawings" (1834), *Latticed Window at Lacock Abbey* (1835, earliest surviving negative), calotype (1841), *The Pencil of Nature* (1844–46). His tiny cameras were called "mousetraps." Note the title: the pencil *of nature* — the hylomorphic dream of matter drawing itself.
- **Sir John Herschel** — coins "photography," "negative," "positive" (1839); cyanotype (1842). **Anna Atkins** (1843) — first photographically illustrated book (cyanotype algae).
- **Joseph Petzval** (1840) — the first mathematically designed fast portrait lens (f/3.6); its off-axis "swirl" is now a sought-after noise.
- **Hippolyte Bayard** — *Self-Portrait as a Drowned Man* (1840), the first staged photograph.
- **Oscar Rejlander**, *The Two Ways of Life* (1857); **Henry Peach Robinson**, *Fading Away* (1858) — combination printing: compositing is as old as the medium.
- **Armand Sabattier** (1862) — the Sabattier effect (solarization), an accident later exploited by Man Ray.
- **Eadweard Muybridge**, **Étienne-Jules Marey** — chronophotography.
- **Public camera obscuras** — Edinburgh (1835, Maria Short), Bristol Clifton (1829), Aberystwyth (1985), San Francisco Giant Camera (1946), Santa Monica (1898/1955). Still-operating two-stage cameras you can stand inside.
- **Kodak Brownie** (1900) — "You press the button, we do the rest": the democratization and the black-boxing of stage two.

### View camera lineage (the acrylic's direct ancestors)
- **Ground glass** — frosted glass at the image plane; the image is viewed, inverted, under a dark cloth.
- **Bellows**, **front/rear standards**, **rail** — independent movement of lens and screen.
- **Movements** — rise/fall, shift, tilt, swing. **Theodor Scheimpflug** (patent 1904) — the Scheimpflug principle: lens plane, image plane, and plane of focus intersect in a common line. Tilting the acrylic is a Scheimpflug demonstration.
- **Film holder** replaces the ground glass at the same plane; **Polaroid** (Edwin Land, 1948) collapses capture and development.
- **Petzval**, **Dallmeyer**, **Cooke Triplet** (1893), **Tessar** (1902), **Sonnar**, **Planar** — lens designs whose signatures (swirl, glow, softness) are today's "character."

### The short recording era, and its end
- **Oskar Barnack, Leica** (1925) — 35mm; the camera becomes fast and small.
- **Kubrick, *Barry Lyndon*** (1975) — Zeiss Planar 50mm f/0.7 (NASA lens) for candlelit scenes; lens speed as aesthetic decision.
- **Gregg Toland, *Citizen Kane*** (1941) — deep focus via small apertures and hard light; depth of field as storytelling.
- **Boyle & Smith, CCD** (Bell Labs, 1969; Nobel 2009). **Steven Sasson, Kodak** (1975) — first digital camera prototype (0.01 MP, 23 s to write to cassette). **Sony Mavica** (1981). **Photoshop 1.0** (1990, Thomas & John Knoll; "Jennifer in Paradise").
- **Smartphone computational photography** (c. 2016–) — HDR stacking, "night mode," semantic segmentation: the camera is now a stage-two agent (an interpreter) even before you touch the file. Fold this into thesis 10.

### Contemporary camera-obscura artists — Artworks tab
- **Abelardo Morell** — since 1991, converts hotel rooms into camera obscuras and photographs the projected city on the walls (long exposures). The canonical contemporary reference. Later uses a tent camera projecting onto the ground.
- **Vera Lutter** — room- and shipping-container-sized pinhole cameras; days-long exposures; presents the negative.
- **Zoe Leonard** — camera-obscura installations (e.g., *945 Madison Avenue*, Whitney 2014; Venice 2012) *(verify titles)*.
- **Chris Fraser** — walk-in light installations.
- **Steven Pippin** — turned a laundromat's washing machines into cameras (*Laundromat-Locomotion*, 1997).
- **Hiroshi Sugimoto**, *Theaters* (since 1976/78) — exposes a whole film's duration onto one frame: a screen photographed as a screen.
- **Marja Pirilä**, **Shi Guorui**, **Dick Tijsma** — other room-camera artists *(verify)*.

## 3. The two-stage system and its descendants — Concepts / Artworks

- **Rear projection** — image thrown onto a translucent screen, viewed from the other side. Cinema process shots (*North by Northwest*), theatre, and your acrylic.
- **Kinescope / telecine** (1947–) — film camera pointed at a TV monitor to archive broadcasts; TV history survives because someone rephotographed a screen.
- **Rephotography (appropriation)** — **Sherrie Levine**, *After Walker Evans* (1981); **Richard Prince**, Marlboro cowboys (c. 1980–). Not to be confused with the **Rephotographic Survey Project** (Mark Klett, 1977–79), re-shooting 19th-c. survey views.
- **Photographing screens** — **Thomas Ruff**, *jpegs* (2004–); **Penelope Umbrico**, *Suns from Flickr* (2006–); **Doug Rickard**, *A New American Picture* (2010–12) and **Jon Rafman**, *9-Eyes* (2008–) — Google Street View shot from the monitor; **Michael Wolf**, *A Series of Unfortunate Events* (2010).
- **Screenshot, screen recording, "phone photo of a monitor"** — the vernacular two-stage capture everyone already does.
- **Hito Steyerl's poor image** as the theory of all of the above.
- **Diffusion models** — start from Gaussian noise, iteratively denoise toward an image conditioned on text (Sohl-Dickstein et al. 2015; Ho et al., DDPM, 2020). Literally noise-become-picture. Seeds as the "grain" of the medium.

## 4. After the capture — processing lineage

- **Tracing → underdrawing → painting** — the 17th-c. workflow. Camera obscura outlines as underdrawing; colour and tone by hand.
- **Negative → positive** — Talbot's insight: the captured image is inverted in tone; the print is a second operation. Inversion is the oldest post-process.
- **Darkroom manipulations** — dodging and burning; contrast grades; **push/pull processing**; **cross-processing**; **solarization/Sabattier** (Man Ray & Lee Miller, 1929); **photograms** (Talbot; Christian Schad's *Schadographs*, 1918/19; Man Ray's *Rayographs*, 1922; Moholy-Nagy, 1922); **combination printing** (Rejlander, Robinson); **Jerry Uelsmann** — multi-negative composites (1960s), Photoshop before Photoshop.
- **Direct-on-film cinema** — **Man Ray**, *Le Retour à la raison* (1923; salt, pins, film exposed without a camera); **Len Lye**, *A Colour Box* (1935); **Norman McLaren**; **Stan Brakhage**, *Mothlight* (1963; moth wings and leaves pressed between film strips). Stage two with no stage one.
- **Digital** — curves, levels, inversion, channel operations, compositing, masking; LUTs; **Photoshop** (1990). The "filter" as an inherited darkroom metaphor.
- **Warhol's silkscreens** — misregistration and inking accidents kept as the work.
- **Marcel Duchamp / Man Ray**, *Dust Breeding* (*Élevage de poussière*, 1920) — a year of dust on *The Large Glass*, photographed: time and accident as material.

## 5. Noise, feedback, resistance — Sound & Noise tab

### Music and recording
- **Luigi Russolo**, *The Art of Noises* (1913) — Futurist manifesto; the *intonarumori*.
- **Pierre Schaeffer**, *Études de bruits* (1948) — *musique concrète*; the *sillon fermé* (closed groove/loop); *objet sonore*.
- **John Cage** — *Imaginary Landscape No. 1* (1939, variable-speed turntables); *4′33″* (1952); chance operations; "everything we do is music."
- **Les Paul** — multitrack, tape delay (1950s).
- **Karlheinz Stockhausen**, *Gesang der Jünglinge* (1955–56); **Delia Derbyshire / BBC Radiophonic Workshop** (*Doctor Who* theme, 1963).
- **Terry Riley**, *Time Lag Accumulator*; **Steve Reich**, *It's Gonna Rain* (1965), *Come Out* (1966) — phasing from tape machines drifting out of sync: a mechanical imperfection becomes a compositional principle.
- **The Beatles** — *I Feel Fine* (1964; deliberate feedback intro); *Rain* (1966; backwards vocal, discovered when Lennon threaded a tape wrong); *Tomorrow Never Knows* (1966; tape loops, Leslie speaker on vocals); **ADT** (artificial double tracking, invented by Abbey Road engineer Ken Townsend, 1966); *Revolution 9* (1968).
- **Jimi Hendrix** — controlled feedback and fuzz as vocabulary; *Star-Spangled Banner* at Woodstock (1969); Marshall stack overdrive.
- **The Velvet Underground / Lou Reed**, *Metal Machine Music* (1975); **Glenn Branca**, **Sonic Youth**, **My Bloody Valentine** (*Loveless*, 1991) — noise as texture.
- **Brian Eno** — "The Studio as Compositional Tool" (lecture 1979, published *Down Beat* 1983); *Discreet Music* (1975, tape-delay system); **Oblique Strategies** (with Peter Schmidt, 1975) — cards like "Honor thy error as a hidden intention"; **Frippertronics** (Robert Fripp); *Ambient 1: Music for Airports* (1978); "generative music." Also his remark that the hiss and distortion of a medium become its beloved features once the medium is superseded.
- **Dub** — **King Tubby**, **Lee "Scratch" Perry** — the mixing desk as instrument; spring reverb, tape echo, dropouts.
- **Hip-hop turntablism** — the scratch as a misuse of the record.
- **Alvin Lucier**, *I Am Sitting in a Room* (1969) — a speech recorded, played back into the room, re-recorded, repeated until only the room's resonant frequencies remain. **The audio twin of the camera-at-monitor activity**: recursion reveals the transfer function.
- **William Basinski**, *The Disintegration Loops* (2001–02) — tape loops flaking apart during digitization; the decay is the piece.
- **Oval (Markus Popp)** — skipping CDs (1990s); **Yasunao Tone**, *Solo for Wounded CD*; **Christian Marclay** — broken records.
- **Kim Cascone**, "The Aesthetics of Failure: 'Post-Digital' Tendencies in Contemporary Computer Music" (*Computer Music Journal*, 2000) — the theoretical statement of glitch.
- **Circuit bending** (Reed Ghazala, 1960s–); **Larsen effect** — acoustic feedback, named for Søren Larsen.
- **Lo-fi** as a genre; **tape saturation**, **wow and flutter**, **vinyl crackle** as plugins — noise fetishized after its medium dies (Eno's point).

### Visual noise and glitch
- **Film grain**, **ISO noise**, **light leaks**, **expired film**, **cross-processing**, **Holga** (1982) / **Lomography** (LC-A, movement founded Vienna 1992) — "Don't think, just shoot."
- **Bill Morrison**, *Decasia* (2002) — decomposing nitrate film as the subject.
- **Tony Conrad**, *Yellow Movies* (1972–73) and pickled/cooked films.
- **Nam June Paik**, *Magnet TV* (1965); **Wolf Vostell**, TV *dé-coll/age* (1963) — distorting the broadcast signal.
- **Steina & Woody Vasulka** — video signal as material; **Rutt/Etra Scan Processor** (1973); Steina's *Violin Power* (1970–78). **The Kitchen** (NYC, 1971).
- **Douglas Trumbull** — slit-scan (*2001*, 1968).
- **Rosa Menkman**, *Glitch Studies Manifesto* (2009/10), *The Glitch Moment/um* (2011). **JODI**. **Nick Briz**. **Daniel Temkin** (*Glitchometry*). **Takeshi Murata**, *Monster Movie* (2005) — datamoshing; Kanye West, *Welcome to Heartbreak* video (2009, dir. Nabil).
- **Databending** — opening an image in a text or audio editor and back.
- **Compression artifacts** — JPEG blocks, macroblocking, ringing, banding; **moiré** (relevant: phone sensor vs. acrylic frosting or screen pixels).
- **Vignetting, flare, ghosting, chromatic aberration, Petzval swirl, halation** — lens "faults" now sold as looks.

## 6. Recursion / mise-en-abyme — Recursion tab

- **Mise en abyme** — from heraldry (a shield within a shield); **André Gide** applies it to literature (journal, 1893).
- **Droste effect** — the 1904 Droste cocoa tin; **Pink Floyd, *Ummagumma*** (1969) cover; **Land O'Lakes** box.
- **Jan van Eyck**, *Arnolfini Portrait* (1434) — the convex mirror; **Velázquez**, *Las Meninas* (1656); **M. C. Escher**, *Drawing Hands* (1948), *Print Gallery* (1956).
- **Ouroboros**; **strange loops** — **Douglas Hofstadter**, *Gödel, Escher, Bach* (1979).
- **Feedback** — Wiener, Bateson (above); positive vs. negative feedback; **Larsen effect** (audio); **video feedback** — camera pointed at its own monitor produces spirals, tunnels, mandalas; the delay of one frame and the monitor's phosphor/LCD response are the "transfer function."
- **Skip Sweeney** and early-70s video feedback artists; **Video Free America**.
- **Nam June Paik**, *TV Buddha* (1974) — a Buddha statue watches its own live image on closed-circuit TV. **The canonical artwork for the recursion activity.**
- **Dan Graham**, *Present Continuous Past(s)* (1974) — mirrors plus an 8-second video delay; **Bruce Nauman**, *Live-Taped Video Corridor* (1970); **Peter Campus**, *Interface* (1972); **Bill Viola**, *Reverse Television* (1983–84).
- **Rosalind Krauss**, "Video: The Aesthetics of Narcissism" (*October*, 1976) — the essay that theorized closed-circuit video as a mirror.
- **Yayoi Kusama**, *Infinity Mirror Room — Phalli's Field* (1965); **Lucas Samaras**, *Mirrored Room* (1966).
- **David Cronenberg, *Videodrome*** (1983); *Inception*'s mirror scene; Hitchcock's *Vertigo* dolly-zoom (not recursion, but a lens-body relationship students should see).
- **Screen burn-in**, **the "hall of mirrors" bug**, **Conway's Life** — recursion in computation.

## 7. Optics and craft — Technical Terms tab

### Camera obscura / lens
- **Camera obscura** — Latin "dark room"; *camera* from Greek *kamara*, vaulted chamber. Italian *stanza oscura*.
- **Aperture**, **stop / diaphragm**, **f-number** (focal length ÷ aperture diameter), **T-stop**.
- **Pinhole** vs. **lens**; **pinhole optimum diameter** (Rayleigh); **diffraction**.
- **Focal length**; **image distance**; **thin-lens equation** (1/f = 1/u + 1/v); **magnification**.
- **Image plane / focal plane / film plane**; **ground glass**; **Fresnel screen**.
- **Focus**; **circle of confusion**; **depth of field**; **hyperfocal distance**; **bokeh** (term popularized in *Photo Techniques*, 1997).
- **Inversion / lateral reversal**; why viewfinders add mirrors and prisms (**pentaprism**, 1949 Contax S).
- **Image circle / coverage**; **vignetting** (optical vs. mechanical); **light falloff**; **hot spot** (rear-projection screens are brightest on-axis).
- **Aberrations** — spherical, chromatic (longitudinal/lateral), coma, astigmatism, field curvature, distortion (barrel/pincushion). **Petzval swirl**, **halation**, **flare**, **ghosting**.
- **Scheimpflug principle**; **tilt/shift**; **swing**; **rise/fall**.
- **Exposure triangle** — aperture, shutter/time, sensitivity (ISO); **reciprocity**; **inverse-square law**.
- **Light leak**; **light-tight**; **dark cloth**; **flocking**.
- **Rear projection**; **translucent vs. transparent**; **diffusion**; **gain** (of a screen).
- **Moiré**; **aliasing**; **Nyquist**; **rolling shutter**; **banding** (LED/screen refresh vs. shutter — relevant for the monitor-recursion activity).

### Lighting (for the basic-skills block)
- **Three-point lighting** — key, fill, back (hair/rim). **Key-to-fill ratio**; **high-key / low-key**.
- **Rembrandt lighting** (triangle on the shadow cheek), **butterfly/Paramount**, **loop**, **split**, **broad vs. short**.
- **Hard vs. soft light**; **source size vs. distance**; **diffusion**, **bounce**, **flag**, **scrim**, **cookie/gobo**, **barn doors**, **snoot**.
- **Motivated light**; **practicals**.
- **Color temperature (K)**, **white balance**, **CRI / TLCI**, **gels (CTO/CTB)**.
- **Chiaroscuro** — Caravaggio, Rembrandt; **George Hurrell** (Hollywood glamour); **Gordon Willis** ("Prince of Darkness," *The Godfather*); **Néstor Almendros** (*Days of Heaven*, natural light); **Vittorio Storaro**; **Roger Deakins**.

### Camera support
- **Tripod** — legs, head (ball, fluid, pan-tilt), **quick-release plate**, **center column**, **spreader**; **monopod**; **hi-hat**; **slider**, **jib**, **dolly**, **gimbal**.
- **Steadicam** (Garrett Brown, 1975); **Louma crane**.
- **Dolly zoom / "Vertigo effect"** (Irmin Roberts, *Vertigo*, 1958).
